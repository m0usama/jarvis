Moving to today's learning moment: chunking strategies and why retrieval fails. 

You already understand how embeddings represent semantic meaning and how vector databases retrieve stored text using similarity scores. But retrieval augmented generation systems almost never fail because of the math in vector similarity. They fail because of how the source text was chopped up before embedding, a process called chunking.

Chunking means dividing long documents into smaller text passages that fit inside an embedding model's context window. If your chunks are too large, distinct ideas get averaged together, diluting the embedding and making it impossible for the vector database to retrieve precise facts. If your chunks are too small, the surrounding context is destroyed, meaning the retrieved chunk contains half a thought that makes no sense to the generating model.

Consider a practical example from product data. Suppose you have a print equipment manual or a catalogue entry. If you use a naive fixed-character chunker of two hundred characters, a sentence like "Maximum sheet thickness for three hundred gram card is zero point four millimetres, but never exceed zero point two millimetres on textured stock" could easily be cut right down the middle. One chunk gets the first clause, the next chunk gets the warning. When a query asks for the limit on textured stock, retrieval might return only the first chunk, causing the model to generate an incorrect, damaging operational instruction.

In real engineering work, robust systems move away from arbitrary character splitting. Instead, they use recursive character chunking that respects natural document boundaries like paragraphs, headings, or markdown tables. For structured files, engineers use semantic or document-aware chunking, ensuring that an entire product specification or business rule remains intact within a single chunk, accompanied by clean metadata. When you design chunking, you are directly engineering retrieval accuracy.
